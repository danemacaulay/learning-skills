# Empirical evidence on LLM and human tutoring (2011–2026), applied to the learning-ground → plan → teach → graph skills

Compiled 2026-11-09. Conventions used below:

- **[RCT]** randomized experiment; **[QE]** quasi-experiment; **[OBS]** observational/survey; **[META]** meta-analysis or review; **[PRE]** preprint or working paper, not peer reviewed; **[POS]** position paper; **[BENCH]** model benchmark with no human learners.
- **Verification level.** "Abstract-verified" means the number was read from the paper's abstract, publisher page, PMC full text, or arXiv record during this research. "Secondary only" means the number was seen only in a summary of the paper. "Not re-fetched" means the work is a well-known classic whose bibliographic details I am confident of but whose text I did not open in this session; no effect size is quoted from such sources unless stated.
- No study found tests this repo or LearnHarness. Every implication is an inference from adjacent evidence.

## 1. Baselines: what did pre-LLM tutoring research establish about step granularity and who should generate?

### Takeaway
The "2 sigma" promise is not a sound baseline: modern estimates put human tutoring at roughly 0.4–0.8 SD and step-based computer tutoring about level with it. Step-level interaction with feedback is where most of the benefit arrives, and the tutoring literature (Chi 2001, ICAP) attributes learning to what the learner constructs, not to how well the tutor explains.

### Cited Findings
- **Bloom's 2-sigma claim has not replicated. [META/critique]** von Hippel's review reports that of 65 tutoring studies in the review Bloom's claim is usually compared to, only one (a 1972 dissertation, 32 students) showed a two-sigma effect; Bloom's source dissertations combined tutoring with mastery learning and extra testing/feedback, which von Hippel estimates accounts for a large part of the effect (secondary summary says "about half"). — [von Hippel 2024, Education Next 24(2)](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/). Original claim: Bloom, B. S. (1984), "The 2 Sigma Problem", Educational Researcher 13(6), 4–16, [doi:10.3102/0013189X013006004](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004) (not re-fetched).
- **Human tutoring, modern pooled estimate: 0.37 SD. [META]** 96 randomized evaluations of PreK–12 tutoring; pooled effect 0.37 SD; none produced two sigma (the last point is von Hippel's reading). — [Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan 2020, NBER WP 27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476)
- **VanLehn 2011: human tutoring d = 0.79, ITS d = 0.76 vs no tutoring. [META/review]** The abstract states the commonly believed values (answer-based 0.3, ITS 1.0, human 2.0) were not confirmed; "the effect size of human tutoring was much lower than previously thought: d = 0.79" and ITS was 0.76, "nearly as effective as human tutoring" (abstract-verified). Step-based tutoring mean 0.76 across 28 comparisons is confirmed from VanLehn's own slides; substep-based ≈ 0.40 is from a secondary summary. — [VanLehn 2011, Educational Psychologist 46(4), 197–221, doi:10.1080/00461520.2011.611369](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369); [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764); [VanLehn slides](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf)
- **Kulik & Fletcher 2016: median ITS effect 0.66 SD. [META]** 50 controlled evaluations; the median (not mean) effect was to raise test scores 0.66 SD over conventional instruction. Effects were smaller on standardized tests than on locally developed tests aligned with the tutor (this last point is from my prior knowledge of the paper, not re-verified). — [Kulik & Fletcher 2016, Review of Educational Research 86(1), 42–78](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review)
- **Ma et al. 2014: ITS ≈ human one-to-one tutoring. [META]** 107 effect sizes, 14,321 participants. No significant difference between ITS and individualized human tutoring (g = −.11) or small-group instruction (g = .05) (abstract-verified). Advantage over large-group teacher-led instruction is reported as g = .44 in an APA summary; my recollection of the abstract is g = .42, so cite the paper directly before quoting either figure. — [Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu 2014, Journal of Educational Psychology 106(4), 901–918, doi:10.1037/a0037123](http://dx.doi.org/10.1037/a0037123)
- **Chi et al. 2001: tutor explanations are not the active ingredient. [Lab experiments]** When tutors were barred from giving explanations and feedback and told to prompt instead, students "learned just as effectively"; learning in the interactive style came from more and deeper scaffolding episodes and students taking more control. — [Chi, Siler, Jeong, Yamauchi & Hausmann 2001, Cognitive Science 25(4), 471–533](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0364-0213(01)00044-1)
- **ICAP: Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive. [Theory + review]** Learning is predicted to increase as overt engagement moves from receiving, to manipulating, to generating new output, to co-generating in dialogue. — [Chi & Wylie 2014, Educational Psychologist 49(4), 219–243, doi:10.1080/00461520.2014.965823](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823). A search result reported a 2023 experimental test that found the opposite ordering; I could not identify the citation, so treat ICAP's ordering as well supported but not unchallenged.
- **Feeling of learning diverges from learning. [RCT, crossover]** In Harvard intro physics, students in active sessions learned more but *felt* they learned less than under polished lectures; authors attribute this partly to the greater cognitive effort of active learning. — [Deslauriers, McCarty, Miller, Callaghan & Kestin 2019, PNAS 116(39), 19251–19257, doi:10.1073/pnas.1821936116](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)
- **Retrieval practice and spacing are robust. [META]** Practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions (Adesope et al.); recall tests give larger benefits than recognition tests (Rowland); distributed practice is reviewed in Cepeda et al. Effect sizes were not verified in this session. — [Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, RER 87(3)](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Rowland 2014, Psychological Bulletin 140(6)](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 354–380 (not re-fetched).

### Inferences
- The repo's core loop (one step → check → repair before building) is the same shape as VanLehn's step-based tutoring, the granularity at which computer tutors matched humans. This is the best-supported part of the design.
- VanLehn's finding that going finer than steps did not add benefit (substep ≈ 0.40 vs step ≈ 0.76, secondary-sourced; he calls this the "interaction plateau", a term I did not see in the abstract I fetched) cautions against the repo's rule "if it needs two explanations, it is two steps" being pushed to ever-smaller steps. Finer is not monotonically better.
- Chi 2001 and ICAP imply the learner should produce the content of a step wherever they can. In the repo, "predict before revealing" is *optional* and the default is tutor exposition followed by a check. In LearnHarness itself the README describes predict → explanation → check as the normal sequence. The evidence favours making learner generation the default for derivable steps, not an option.
- Deslauriers 2019 is a direct challenge to the terminal condition "the goal feels obviously true and the learner confirms". Felt self-evidence after fluent explanation is exactly the signal shown to be miscalibrated. Completion should be evidenced by unaided performance.
- The repo has "no practice skill". The testing/spacing literature says a single same-session check per step is the weakest form of retrieval practice (immediate, massed). LearnHarness has separate practice sessions with varied problems; the repo dropped the component with the most robust evidence base.

### Gaps
- VanLehn's per-category values for answer-based tutoring and the exact "interaction plateau" wording were not read from the paper itself.
- No study isolates "one inferential step" as the unit of instruction in conceptual/humanities domains; step-based ITS evidence is overwhelmingly from maths, physics and programming problem solving.

## 2. LLM-era RCTs and field studies: what separates helpful from harmful conditions?

### Takeaway
The pattern across the stronger trials is consistent: LLM tutors help when they withhold answers, follow a pre-authored sequence, are grounded in vetted content, and are measured on unaided tests; open chat access raises assisted performance and lowers or leaves unchanged unaided performance. Pooled meta-analytic "large effects" are not trustworthy: the most-cited one was retracted in 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Bastani et al. 2025 (PNAS). [RCT, field, ~1,000 Turkish high-school maths students]** GPT-4 access during practice raised practice grades 48% (GPT Base, ChatGPT-like) and 127% (GPT Tutor, prompted with safeguards and teacher-written hints). When access was removed, GPT Base students scored 17% *worse* than students who never had access; GPT Tutor's safeguards "largely mitigated" the harm, i.e. GPT Tutor students ended about level with control, not ahead (abstract-verified). — [Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman 2025, PNAS 122(26) e2422633122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
- **Kestin et al. 2025 (Scientific Reports). [RCT, crossover, N = 194 Harvard intro physics students, two lessons]** Median post-test 4.5 (AI tutor) vs 3.5 (in-class active learning), baseline 2.75; z = −5.6, p < 10⁻⁸; effect 0.63 SD by linear regression, 0.73–1.3 SD by quantile regression; median time on task 49 min vs 60 min. The tutor was built on seven practices: active learning, cognitive load management, growth mindset, scaffolding, accuracy, timely feedback, self-pacing. A system prompt alone "could not reliably provide enough structure", so the platform walked students through each problem part in sequence, and prompts were "enriched … with comprehensive, step-by-step answers" because the authors would not rely on GPT-4 alone for correctness. No delayed retention test; one course; the authors built the tutor. (PMC full text.) — [Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti 2025, Sci Rep 15, 17458](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
  - Contested: an independent re-analysis argues "over double" is overstated and the effect shrinks when only students present in both conditions are analysed. — [Ford, re-analysis blog](https://www.clayford.net/posts/ai_tutoring_nature/) (not peer reviewed)
- **Tutor CoPilot. [RCT, preregistered, PRE; 900 tutors, 1,800 K-12 students]** Students whose human tutors had LLM-generated expert-like suggestions were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics (p < 0.01), up to 9 p.p. for lower-rated tutors. Analysis of 550,000+ messages: treated tutors asked more guiding questions and were less likely to give away answers. — [Wang, Ribeiro, Robinson, Loeb & Demszky 2024/2025, arXiv:2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017)
- **Lehmann et al. [Two preregistered lab RCTs, PRE; coding]** No significant average effect of LLM access on learning. Students who used the LLM as a *substitute* (having it generate solutions) covered more topics with shallower understanding; those who used it as a *complement* (asking for explanations) understood more. Students prefer substitution, and LLM access widened the gap between low and high prior knowledge. — [Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting, arXiv:2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)
- **Nigeria (World Bank). [RCT, PRE; six-week after-school programme, Edo State]** Teacher-guided sessions with Microsoft Copilot/GPT-4: 0.31 SD on the overall assessment, 0.23 SD on English. — [De Simone et al. 2025, World Bank Policy Research WP 11125](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee/full)
- **Ghana, Rori maths tutor. [Cluster QE/RCT, PRE; ~1,000 students, 11 schools, 8 months]** Effect 0.37 on maths growth (p < 0.001); 0.36 in another version. School-level assignment with few clusters; year-1 results only. — [Henkel, Horne-Robinson, Kozhakhmetova & Lee 2024, arXiv:2402.09809](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09809)
- **LearnLM in UK classrooms. [Exploratory RCT, PRE; N = 165, 5 schools; vendor-run]** Human tutors supervised every LearnLM message; 76.4% of drafts were sent with zero or minimal edits (so roughly a quarter needed changing). Students did at least as well as with human-only tutoring; success on novel problems in later topics was 66.2% vs 60.7% (+5.5 p.p.; abstract gives no CI). Tutors praised its Socratic questions. — [LearnLM Team & Eedi 2025, arXiv:2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)
- **Google "Towards responsible development". [Evaluation framework, PRE]** LearnLM-Tutor (fine-tuned Gemini 1.0) was preferred by educators and learners over a prompt-tuned Gemini on several pedagogy dimensions across seven benchmarks; the paper stresses that the field lacks a shared way to measure pedagogy. Preference studies are not learning outcomes. — [Jurenka et al. 2024, arXiv:2407.12687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12687)
- **Khanmigo. [Cluster RCT, PRE; 18 Tennessee middle schools, two years]** +1.3 national percentile ranks per term ≈ 0.06–0.08 SD per year; 0.14 SD implied for full active use; similar to Khan Academy practice without AI. Students messaged the tutor in only 17% of sessions where they made a mistake, mostly with bare answers or suggested-prompt clicks. "Realizing the promise of AI tutoring will require getting students to use it." — [Oreopoulos & Low 2026, NBER WP 35620](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620)
- **Pardos & Bhandari 2024. [RCT, N = 274, PLOS ONE]** ChatGPT-written hints produced learning gains statistically equal to human-authored hints in algebra/statistics, *after* hints were filtered: 32% of ChatGPT hints failed quality checks (abstract-verified); self-consistency reduced errors to near 0% for algebra and 13% for statistics (secondary only). — [Pardos & Bhandari 2024, PLOS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0304013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0304013)
- **Meta-analyses of ChatGPT on learning are unreliable.**
  - Wang & Fan 2025 (g = 0.867, 51 studies) was **retracted 22 April 2026** for "discrepancies in the meta-analysis"; critics Ingebrigtsen & Lukic reported a retracted study among the inputs, mislabelled studies and implausible effects. Do not cite its effect size. — original [doi:10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y); retraction coverage: [NEPC](https://nepc.colorado.edu/node/14336), [Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/07/15/study-claiming-ai-helps-students-learn-retracted/) (I did not open the retraction notice itself).
  - Deng et al. 2025 (69 experimental studies, positive effects on performance and higher-order thinking, reduced mental effort) acknowledges weak primary studies. — [Deng, Jiang, Yu, Lu & Liu 2025, Computers & Education 227, 105224](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224)
  - Weidlich, Gašević, Drachsler & Kirschner audited a subset of Deng et al.'s primary studies against three requirements (treatment described, control activity described, outcome is a valid measure of learning); "only a small minority" met all three, so effects "cannot, at this time, be confidently attributed to ChatGPT". — [Weidlich et al. 2025, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 41, e70105](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/chatgpt-in-education-an-effect-in-search-of-a-cause/)
  - Programming-specific meta-analysis: no significant effect of GenAI on learning outcomes, g = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.47], 23 studies. **[META, PRE]** — [Maier et al. 2026, arXiv:2605.04779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04779)
  - A meta-meta-analysis preprint (Bartoš et al., 1,840 effects from 67 meta-analyses) reportedly finds severe publication bias with bias-adjusted effects much smaller and prediction intervals spanning zero. **Secondary only**; I did not locate the preprint. — [news summary](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/barto-et-al-find-ai-education-gains-shrink-under-bias-controls)

### Inferences
Design features that recur in the positive conditions, and where the repo stands:

| Feature in helpful conditions | Evidence | Repo status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tutor does not hand over the answer; learner attempts first | Bastani (Tutor vs Base); Tutor CoPilot; Lehmann | Partly. Checks follow each step, but the step's content is usually told first; "predict" is optional |
| Externally imposed step sequence, not left to the model in one prompt | Kestin ("system prompt alone could not reliably provide enough structure") | Present: plan.md plus one-step-one-check hard rule |
| Content grounded in pre-written, human-vetted solutions | Kestin; Bastani GPT Tutor (teacher hints); Pardos (filtered hints); LearnLM RCT (human approves every message) | **Absent.** Teacher prose is generated from model memory with no network and no review |
| Outcome measured without the AI present, ideally delayed | Bastani's reversal only appeared on the unaided exam | Absent. Completion is learner-confirmed feeling within the session |
| Learner actually engages substantively | Khanmigo null-ish result driven by shallow use | Present for a self-selected adult who invoked the skill; forced reply after each step is a strength |
- All the large positive results are in well-structured STEM or language skills with a known answer key. The repo is being used for interpretive material (a session directory is named for Heidegger), where no trial evidence exists and where "pre-written solutions" have no direct analogue other than source texts.
- Bastani's GPT Tutor ended at parity with no-AI controls on the unaided exam. The realistic expectation for a well-guarded chat tutor versus self-study is "no harm, more efficient", not 2 sigma.

### Gaps
- No RCT of an LLM tutor in philosophy or other interpretive humanities was found.
- No trial of adult self-directed learners using an agent-skill tutor; nearly all samples are school or university students in assigned courses.
- "Stanford/Wharton" in the brief resolves to Tutor CoPilot (Stanford) and Bastani (Wharton); no additional distinct Stanford Nigeria study was found.
- Long-term retention is unmeasured in almost every LLM tutoring trial, including Kestin.

## 3. Over-reliance and cognitive offloading: does "the system owns logistics" conflict with self-regulated-learning evidence?

### Takeaway
Experimental evidence supports that AI lowers effort and can lower depth and self-monitoring; the most publicised claims of cognitive damage (Kosmyna, Gerlich) are weak or contested. The repo's principle is defensible under cognitive load theory for *extraneous* work (sourcing, ordering), but two things it currently offloads, monitoring one's own understanding and verifying claims, are ones the evidence says should stay with, or at least be visible to, the learner.

### Cited Findings
- **"Metacognitive laziness". [RCT, lab, N = 117 university students, BJET]** Four conditions (ChatGPT, human expert, writing-analytics checklist, no tool). ChatGPT group improved essay scores most, with no significant differences in knowledge gain or transfer or in intrinsic motivation; process data showed different self-regulation patterns, which the authors label possible "metacognitive laziness". — [Fan, Tang, Le, Shen, Tan, Zhao, Shen, Li & Gašević 2025, BJET 56(2), 489–530, doi:10.1111/bjet.13544](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)
- **Rely on vs learn from. [RCT, N = 1,625, 10 courses, Computers & Education]** After AI feedback prompts were withdrawn, students' performance on the supported task declined unless a self-monitoring checklist replaced them; authors conclude students "tended to rely on rather than learn from AI assistance". Combining AI with self-regulation scaffolds was no better than AI alone. — [Darvishi, Khosravi, Sadiq, Gašević & Siemens 2024, Computers & Education 210, 104967](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/99e9a0ee-4feb-405c-8e66-062c6b67aa11)
- **Lower load, lower depth. [RCT, N = 91, Computers in Human Behavior]** Students researching a science question with ChatGPT-3.5 reported lower cognitive load than those using Google but produced lower-quality reasoning and argumentation. — [Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024, CHB 160, 108386](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386)
- **Microsoft/CMU survey. [OBS, self-report, 319 knowledge workers, CHI 2025]** Higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more. 58 participants reported difficulty verifying AI output for lack of domain knowledge. Critical effort shifts to verification, integration and "task stewardship". Correlational and self-reported. — [Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al. 2025, CHI '25, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713778](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **Gerlich 2025. [OBS, cross-sectional survey + interviews, N = 666, Societies (MDPI)]** Negative association between AI tool use and a critical-thinking measure, mediated by cognitive offloading. Cannot support causal claims; self-selected, self-report. — [Gerlich 2025, Societies 15(1), 6, doi:10.3390/soc15010006](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006)
- **Kosmyna et al. "Your Brain on ChatGPT". [PRE, lab, N = 54; 18 in the final session]** Reports weaker EEG connectivity and poorer recall of own essays among LLM users, framed as "cognitive debt". **Contested:** a formal comment criticises sample size, reproducibility, EEG analysis methods and ambiguous significance criteria; "cognitive debt" is not an established construct. — [Kosmyna et al. 2025, arXiv:2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872); [Stankovic, Hirche, Kollatzsch & Doetsch 2026, arXiv:2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856)
- **Which self-regulation processes matter. [META, k = 430, N = 90,380, Psychological Bulletin]** Goal level, persistence, effort and self-efficacy had the strongest effects on learning (17% of variance after ability and prior knowledge); planning, monitoring, help seeking and emotion control did *not* show significant relationships with learning. Mostly higher-education and workplace training. — [Sitzmann & Ely 2011, Psychological Bulletin 137(3), 421–442](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/)
- **SRL-scaffolded LLM tutors. [RCT, preregistered crossover, N = 1,059, CS1; ICER 2026]** Tutors built on Zimmerman's cycle were compared with a baseline AI tutor; the title reports that more engagement did not mean more learning. I saw only the listing, not the results. — [ICER 2026 listing](https://icer2026.acm.org/details/icer-2026-papers/18/When-More-Engagement-Doesn-t-Mean-More-Learning-LLM-Tutors-and-Self-Regulated-Learni)
- **SRL theory (for framing, not effect sizes).** Zimmerman's cyclical model (forethought → performance → self-reflection) and Winne & Hadwin's model (task definition → goals/plans → tactics → adaptation, with monitoring throughout) treat planning and monitoring as learnable skills. — Zimmerman 2002, Theory Into Practice 41(2), 64–70, [doi:10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2); Winne & Hadwin 1998, "Studying as self-regulated learning", in Hacker, Dunlosky & Graesser (eds.), *Metacognition in Educational Theory and Practice*, 277–304 (both not re-fetched).
- **LearnHarness's own wording of the principle** is broader than the repo's: effort allocation means minimising "planning, sourcing, and fact-checking" for the learner, with the system absorbing them. — [LearnHarness teaching-methodology.md](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/teaching-methodology.md)

### Inferences
- The conflict with SRL evidence is narrower than it first appears. Sitzmann & Ely found planning and monitoring *as measured* did not predict learning once goals and effort were accounted for, and goal level did. The repo keeps the goal with the learner (grounding clarifies it; the learner approves the plan). Offloading sequencing and research to the system is consistent with cognitive load theory's advice to remove extraneous load, and Kestin's tutor did exactly this with a positive result.
- Two caveats. First, Stadler shows AI can reduce *germane* load as well as extraneous load; "logistics" must not expand to include the reasoning itself. Second, Fan and Darvishi show that when the AI does the monitoring, learners stop monitoring. In the repo the tutor decides whether a check "took" and whether the goal is reached; the learner is never asked to judge their own understanding *before* feedback.
- The repo's "system owns … research" inherits LearnHarness's "sourcing and fact-checking", but the repo then performs neither (no network, no verification pass). The learner is relieved of fact-checking and nobody else does it. Lee et al. found verification is where human critical effort relocates under AI and that people without domain knowledge cannot do it, which describes a learner by definition.
- Kosmyna and Gerlich should not be used to argue for or against the design; neither can carry a causal claim.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that compares a learner-made plan with an AI-made plan on learning outcomes. A plan-quality comparison exists (Goslen et al., per a search result) but without learning outcomes.
- No evidence on whether skill at planning one's own learning atrophies after sustained use of AI planners.
- I did not verify the ICER 2026 or Fischer, Rau & Rilke (IZA DP 18338, N = 334, immediate vs delayed AI-tutor access) results beyond search snippets.

## 4. Socratic vs expository: when should the tutor tell and when should it ask?

### Takeaway
Neither pure style is supported. The literature converges on a conditional rule: tell when the learner lacks the prior knowledge to generate the step (novices, arbitrary facts), ask or have them attempt first when they can plausibly reach it, and fade guidance as they advance. LLMs left to defaults err toward telling and toward revealing answers early.

### Cited Findings
- **The assistance dilemma. [Review of Cognitive Tutor experiments]** The question of when to give and when to withhold information is not resolved by choosing a side; it requires specifying the dimension of assistance and tuning it to the learner and content. — [Koedinger & Aleven 2007, Educational Psychology Review 19, 239–264, doi:10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0)
- **Expertise reversal. [Review]** Guidance that helps novices (worked examples, integrated explanations) becomes redundant or harmful as knowledge grows; instruction should be adjusted as expertise develops. — [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, Educational Psychologist 38(1), 23–31](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136); [Kalyuga 2007, Educational Psychology Review 19, 509–539, doi:10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- **Productive failure. [META, 53 studies, 166 comparisons]** Problem solving before instruction beats instruction first on conceptual understanding and transfer, g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]; g = 0.37–0.58 when productive-failure design principles are followed. The advantage disappears or reverses for younger learners (grades 2–5) and for domain-general skills. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research 91(5), doi:10.3102/00346543211019105](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- **Prompting can substitute for explaining. [Lab]** See Chi et al. 2001 in section 1: suppressing tutor explanations did not reduce learning. — [Chi et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0364-0213(01)00044-1)
- **LLMs reveal answers too early. [BENCH + teacher study]** MathDial (3k teacher–student dialogues) was motivated by the finding that LLM tutors "generate factually incorrect feedback or are prone to revealing solutions to students too early"; fine-tuned models told solutions less often at similar student success. — [Macina et al. 2023, Findings of EMNLP, arXiv:2305.14536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14536)
- **Withholding answers changes outcomes. [RCT]** Bastani's GPT Tutor (hints, no answers) removed the unaided-exam penalty seen with plain chat; Tutor CoPilot shifted human tutors toward guiding questions with a mastery gain. — [Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [Wang et al., arXiv:2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017)
- **Socratic style has a perceived-efficiency cost.** Search results describe a 66-student programming study where a Socratic tutor produced higher learning gains than a prompt-refinement tutor but was rated less efficient, and a Kassel study (n = 36 vs 29) with self-report outcomes only. I could not verify either paper's citation details, so they are noted as leads, not findings. Kestin et al. explicitly list "when the AI tutor should openly provide answers versus guiding students to reflect" as an open research question. — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)

### Inferences
- The repo's rule ("Socratic when they can reason there; expository when not"; foundations asserted plainly) matches the conditional rule the literature supports. Keep it. Its weakness is that the choice is left to tutor judgement with no stated criterion, and MathDial shows the default LLM bias is toward telling.
- A usable criterion from the evidence: (a) *unconditional truths* (not derivable from what is held) → assert, then check with an application item; (b) *derivable steps* → learner attempts first (productive failure, Chi 2001), tutor consolidates after; (c) if the attempt shows missing prerequisites → switch to worked explanation (expertise reversal in the novice direction) and revise the plan. Grounding.md already records what is held, which is the information needed to make this call.
- Productive failure requires a consolidation phase after the attempt; "predict, then teach" in the repo already has that shape.
- The "caveat-free" instruction for unconditional truths has no support in this literature and collides with section 5 for contested or interpretive claims.

### Gaps
- No verified head-to-head RCT of Socratic vs direct-explanation LLM tutoring with an unaided, delayed post-test. A two-arm trial on exactly this question was described as in progress.
- Productive-failure evidence is mostly maths/science concepts with school and undergraduate learners.

## 5. Hallucination and correctness: what does the evidence imply for storing unverified teacher prose as durable "true" propositions?

### Takeaway
Every successful LLM tutoring trial that reported on accuracy solved it outside the model (pre-written solutions, filtered hints, a human approving each message). Measured raw error rates in tutoring output are far from negligible, and learners, novices especially, do not reliably catch errors. Writing unverified tutor prose into a persistent graph that future sessions treat as held ground is the least evidence-supported part of the design.

### Cited Findings
- **Raw error rate in tutoring hints: 32%. [RCT materials audit]** 32% of ChatGPT-generated maths hints failed quality checks before filtering (abstract-verified); self-consistency voting reduced this to near 0% (algebra) and 13% (statistics) (secondary only). — [Pardos & Bhandari 2024, PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0304013)
- **Human supervisors changed about a quarter of tutor messages. [RCT, PRE]** With a pedagogy-tuned model, 76.4% of drafts were approved with at most trivial edits; the remainder were revised before students saw them. Edits include pedagogy as well as accuracy, so this is an upper bound on error, not an error rate. — [LearnLM Team & Eedi 2025, arXiv:2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)
- **Successful tutors did not trust model memory. [RCT]** "We avoided relying solely on GPT-4 to generate solutions"; prompts were enriched with step-by-step answers. — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- **LLMs struggle to locate student errors; verifying first helps. [BENCH, EMNLP 2024]** On 1,002 teacher-annotated stepwise solutions, finding the first error was hard for current models; separating verification from response generation produced fewer hallucinations and better-targeted feedback (authors' evaluations). — [Daheim, Macina, Kapur, Gurevych & Sachan 2024, arXiv:2407.09136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09136)
- **Learners adopt wrong AI advice about half the time. [RCT, PRE; high-school students]** With ChatGPT advice wrong on half of eight maths puzzles, incorrect recommendations were adopted in 52.1% of trials. A brief AI-literacy intervention did not significantly reduce over-reliance and increased rejection of *correct* advice. — [Puppart & Aru 2025, arXiv:2503.10556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10556)
- **Errors that match a learner's misconception are caught least. [Lab experiment, N = 124 engineering students]** Using researcher-written feedback simulating LLM explanations, errors congruent with the learner's existing misconception were detected 36% of the time vs 58% for incongruent errors. Search-snippet verified only; authors and venue not confirmed. — [eScholarship record](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9pk053k6)
- **Fabricated citations. [Audit, Scientific Reports]** In 636 citations across 84 generated literature reviews on 42 topics, 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Older models; shows the failure is attribution-specific. — [Walters & Wilder 2023, Sci Rep 13, 14045](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)
- **Verification is hardest for those without domain knowledge. [OBS]** 58 of 319 knowledge workers cited limited domain knowledge as a barrier to verifying AI responses. — [Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **The repo's current rule.** "Teacher prose is the source for graph extraction"; the ground and graph skills declare "No network". (Read from `skills/_shared/folder-layout.md`, `skills/learning-graph/SKILL.md`, `skills/learning-ground/SKILL.md` in the repo.) LearnHarness's theory document assigns fact-checking to the system and tells the tutor to present foundations as "unconditionally true". — [LearnHarness teaching-methodology.md](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/teaching-methodology.md)

### Inferences
- The failure mode compounds across the skill chain. A wrong step that passes its check (the check is "answerable from the session", so it tests agreement with the tutor, not with the world) is recorded in transcript.md, extracted as a node, and pre-read by later sessions as something the learner already holds. The misconception-congruency finding suggests the errors most likely to survive are those the learner already half-believed.
- "Feels obviously true" amplifies this: the method's success criterion is the learner's conviction, so a successful session on a false proposition produces a confidently held false belief. Nothing in the loop distinguishes that from a true one.
- In interpretive domains there is an additional category error. "Heidegger holds X" is an attribution that can be checked against a text; "X is true" is a contested philosophical claim; "the standard reading is X" is a claim about scholarship. Storing all three as flat "propositions" presented "caveat-free" erases distinctions the discipline depends on. No empirical study was found on this specifically; it follows from the citation-fabrication and attribution evidence plus the design.
- Mitigations with at least some evidence: grounding in supplied source material (Kestin; Bastani's teacher hints), a separate verification pass before generation or before storage (Daheim), multiple-sample agreement (Pardos), and a human in the loop for anything persisted (LearnLM/Eedi). The repo's plan-approval step is a natural place to attach sources; graph extraction is a natural place for a verification gate.

### Gaps
- No measured hallucination rate for LLM tutoring in philosophy or the interpretive humanities was found.
- No study measures long-term retention of errors absorbed from an LLM tutor.
- Error rates quoted are for 2023–2025 models; current frontier models are likely better on well-trodden content, but I found no tutoring-specific measurement for 2026 models.
- No evidence was found on persistent knowledge-graph or memory contamination in tutoring systems specifically.

## 6. Sycophancy and check grading: do LLMs over-accept learner answers?

### Takeaway
There is direct benchmark evidence that LLMs shift toward what a "student" asserts and can abandon a correct correction under social pressure, and that locating the actual error in student reasoning is hard for them. Evidence on grading leniency is mixed in direction. Human-learner outcome studies are missing, so this is a credible risk, not a quantified harm.

### Cited Findings
- **Student-suggested answers move the model. [BENCH, PRE, KDD 2025 workshop]** In simulated student queries on MMLU items, mentioning a wrong answer lowered model accuracy by up to 15 percentage points and mentioning the right one raised it similarly; the swing was larger in smaller models (up to ~30% for GPT-4.1-nano vs ~8% for GPT-4o). — [Arvin 2025, "Check My Work?", arXiv:2506.10297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10297)
- **"Pedagogical sycophancy". [POS + BENCH, PRE]** Argues tutoring needs "corrective friction"; introduces EduFrameTrap across six subjects. Reports that frontier models that resist reframing attacks can still retreat under appeals to authority ("my notes say I'm right") and face-saving requests ("please don't tell me I'm wrong"), and that two LLM judges disagree on these cases. The abstract gives no rates; a ~14% figure appears only in a secondary review. — [Kasneci & Kasneci 2026, arXiv:2605.14604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14604)
- **Diagnosing the error is hard; decoupling verification helps. [BENCH]** See Daheim et al. in section 5. — [Daheim et al. 2024, arXiv:2407.09136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09136)
- **Grading bias runs both ways.** One study of 2,358 dental short answers found both models tested over-scored incorrect answers ("optimistic bias") under a 12-point analytic rubric; a mechanical-ventilation study found ChatGPT *under*-scored relative to humans (mean bias −1.34/10; κ ≈ −0.08), with less disagreement on checklist-style rubric items. — [dental study, PMC12896245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12896245); [ventilation study, arXiv:2505.04645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04645) (both read from search summaries only)
- **Rubric structure matters. [BENCH, PRE]** Rubric-conditioned LLM grading aligns well with humans for binary judgements and degrades as rubric granularity increases. — [arXiv:2601.08843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08843)
- **Vetted hints and human review were the working mitigations in trials.** — [Pardos & Bhandari 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0304013); [LearnLM & Eedi 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)

### Inferences
- In the repo, one model in one context writes the explanation, writes the check, reads the learner's reply, decides hit or miss, and decides whether the goal is reached, with the learner's self-report as the final gate. Each of those is a point where the benchmark evidence predicts drift toward acceptance, and the conversational setting (a learner who says "yes, that makes sense") supplies exactly the social pressure Kasneci & Kasneci describe.
- Cheap mitigations consistent with the evidence: write the expected answer and the specific wrong answers (with the misconception each indicates) *before* the learner replies and record them in the transcript; judge the reply against that pre-commitment as a binary took/didn't-take decision (binary rubric grading is where LLM–human agreement is best); do the judging as a distinct step before composing the reply (Daheim's verify-then-generate); optionally use a separate grader context that sees only the criterion and the answer.
- The repo already says to "design misses to name the wrong model". That is the right instinct; it needs to be committed in writing ahead of the answer for it to resist sycophancy.

### Gaps
- No human-subjects study measures how often an LLM tutor wrongly accepts an incorrect learner answer in live tutoring, or what that does to learning.
- No study tests whether pre-committed rubrics or separate graders reduce over-acceptance in tutoring specifically; the inference rests on grading and verification benchmarks.

## 7. Learner control and plan approval: is having the learner approve the plan supported?

### Takeaway
Giving learners control over instructional sequence has, on average, almost no effect on learning, so plan approval should not be expected to improve outcomes by itself; goal-setting, by contrast, is one of the better-supported levers. Approval is cheap and worth keeping for goal commitment and for catching wrong assumptions about what the learner holds, not as a learning mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- **Learner control ≈ zero effect. [META, 18 studies, 29 effects, RER]** Updating Niemiec et al. 1996, the overall effect of learner control in educational technology was near zero (g = 0.05, secondary-sourced figure), small for both academic and behavioural outcomes, and no different from the 1996 conclusion despite newer technology. — [Karich, Burns & Maki 2014, Review of Educational Research 84(3)](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/updated-meta-analysis-of-learner-control-within-educational-techn/fingerprints/); [Education Week coverage](https://edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05)
- **Goal level predicts learning; planning as measured does not. [META]** — [Sitzmann & Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/)
- **Goal-setting theory.** Specific, difficult goals outperform vague "do your best" goals, moderated by commitment and feedback. — Locke & Latham 2002, American Psychologist 57(9), 705–717, [doi:10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) (not re-fetched; no effect size quoted).
- **Novices choose poorly without guidance.** Expertise-reversal work implies low-knowledge learners benefit from system-imposed structure. — [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- **System-imposed sequence worked in the strongest LLM trial.** — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- **Self-pacing was one of Kestin's seven design practices**, i.e. control over pace, not over content sequence. — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)

### Inferences
- The repo's split (system proposes order; learner sets the goal, approves, can edit scope) is close to what the evidence would recommend: keep the goal and pace with the learner, the sequencing with the system.
- A learner who does not yet know the domain cannot evaluate a prerequisite graph for correctness. Approval is therefore not a quality check on the plan. What the learner *can* check is whether the "already held" nodes are really held and whether the goal is the one they meant. The approval prompt should ask those two questions rather than "proceed as-is?".
- The plan stage is the right moment to make the goal specific and testable (what will you be able to do or explain unaided), which serves both goal-setting theory and the need for an unaided end-of-session measure (sections 1–2).

### Gaps
- Learner-control meta-analytic evidence predates LLMs and conversational tutors. A 2025 RCT preprint on an AI instructional agent and perceived learner control surfaced in search (arXiv:2505.22526) but was not read.
- No study found on mermaid/graph-style advance organisers presented by LLM tutors.

## 8. Does LearnHarness itself cite research, and what is its stated rationale?

### Takeaway
No. LearnHarness cites no research anywhere I looked, and its own documentation index says the methodology is the owner's theory, "not … evidence of measured learning outcomes". The repo under review inherits an untested personal theory, in a narrower form than the original.

### Cited Findings
- The README describes the method (measure → plan → teach → practice), states "The aim is for the goal to feel obviously true, not merely familiar", and names no papers, authors or studies. — [LearnHarness README](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness)
- The docs index describes `teaching-methodology.md` as "the owner's theory underlying the teaching method. It is not a runtime specification or evidence of measured learning outcomes." — [LearnHarness docs/README.md](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/README.md)
- `teaching-methodology.md` sets out: two knowledge states; learning as felt self-evidence (memorised-but-not-self-evident facts are said to fade); "the click"; knowledge as a graph; teaching as ordered assertion one reasoning step at a time; foundations that must be unconditionally true; effort allocation (system absorbs "planning, sourcing, and fact-checking"); the minimal tree; one graded check per reasoning step, flagged as a locked decision to "revisit" if it "proves unpleasant in practice". It makes claims about retention and how facts are committed without sources and carries no evidence disclaimer of its own. — [LearnHarness docs/teaching-methodology.md](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/teaching-methodology.md) (summarised via fetch; quotations are short phrases returned by the fetch tool)
- LearnHarness as described in its README includes components the repo dropped or weakened: a predict step before each explanation as standard, "thinking" fields for the learner's own reasoning, and separate practice sessions that "pose varied problems that require the right knowledge to come to mind without being named first". — [LearnHarness README](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness)
- The repo's README states "Teaching only in this cut — no practice skill", and makes predict optional. — local files `README.md`, `skills/learning-teach/SKILL.md`

### Inferences
- Parts of the theory happen to line up with established findings (step-level checking with VanLehn; building from prior knowledge; structure over isolated facts), but the alignment is coincidental, not cited, and the theory's distinctive claims ("felt self-evidence" as the criterion of learning; connected facts are held "by relations, not by rehearsal") run against Deslauriers 2019 and the retrieval-practice literature.
- The distillation removed the two LearnHarness features that the evidence most supports (predict-first and varied, delayed practice) and kept the two it least supports (felt obviousness as the terminal condition; unverified tutor assertion as truth).

### Gaps
- I read LearnHarness's README, docs index and methodology document through a summarising fetch, not the raw files; its agent prompts (`src/agent/prompts/`) and `skills/learnharness/SKILL.md` were not read and may add safeguards not described here.
- No evaluation, user study or issue-tracker evidence about LearnHarness's outcomes was found.

## 9. Evidence-linked implications for this repo's skills

### Takeaway
Keep the step-check-repair loop, the system-owned sequencing, the prior-knowledge grounding and the tell/ask mixture. Change four things the evidence does not support: tutor-first delivery by default, completion by felt obviousness, unverified prose as graph truth, and single-context self-graded checks. Add the practice component the distillation left out.

### Cited Findings
**Keep (evidence supports the existing design)**

1. **One step → one check → repair before building.** Step-based tutoring is the granularity at which computer tutors matched human tutors (d ≈ 0.76 vs 0.79), and Kestin found the sequence had to be enforced by the system, not left to a single prompt. — [VanLehn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369); [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
2. **System owns ordering and sourcing; learner owns the goal.** Learner control of sequence has near-zero effect; goal level is among the strongest self-regulation predictors. — [Karich et al. 2014](https://edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05); [Sitzmann & Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/)
3. **Mixture of telling and asking, chosen by what the learner can derive.** — [Koedinger & Aleven 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0); [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
4. **Checks that apply or predict, not parrot; waiting for a reply every step.** Forced substantive engagement is what Khanmigo's field deployment lacked. — [Oreopoulos & Low 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620); [Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
5. **Graph stores no mastery scores; presence does not prove the learner holds it.** Consistent with keeping learner state separate from domain claims; see change 3 for what the graph should additionally record.

**Change**

1. **Make learner-attempt-first the default for derivable steps (`learning-teach` §2; `theory.md` "Two placement moves").** Promote the optional "predict" to the standard move for motivated derivations; assert-then-check only for unconditional truths or after a failed attempt shows missing prerequisites. — [Chi et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0364-0213(01)00044-1); [Chi & Wylie 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823); [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105); [Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [Macina et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14536)
2. **Replace "goal feels obviously true and the learner confirms" with an unaided performance criterion (`theory.md` "Terminal condition"; `learning-teach` §4).** End with a no-lookback task: the learner reconstructs the chain to the goal, or applies it to a new case, without the transcript. Keep "feels obvious" as a secondary signal only. — [Deslauriers et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116); [Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
3. **Stop treating teacher prose as ground truth (`learning-graph`; `folder-layout.md`; `graph-schema.md`).** Add a verification pass before extraction; give each node a provenance and status field (for example: source-verified with citation / tutor-asserted unverified / contested); distinguish claim types in interpretive domains (attribution to an author with locus, scholarly consensus, contested interpretation). Do not let unverified nodes be pre-read as "held floors". — [Pardos & Bhandari 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0304013); [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/); [Daheim et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09136); [Puppart & Aru 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10556); [Walters & Wilder 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)
4. **Ground the plan in sources (`learning-plan` §2).** Drop "No network required" as a design virtue. Where tools allow, collect and list the primary/secondary sources each landmark rests on in plan.md, or accept learner-supplied texts; teach from those. If run offline, say so in the plan and mark the session's output unverified. — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/); [Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [LearnLM & Eedi 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)
5. **Remove "caveat-free" for unconditional truths (`theory.md`).** State the epistemic status in one clause (definition, established result, the author's claim, one reading among several). The LearnHarness requirement is that foundations be unconditionally true; the tutor cannot guarantee that without verification. — [LearnHarness methodology](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/teaching-methodology.md); [Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
6. **Pre-commit check criteria and separate judging from replying (`learning-teach` §2 steps 4–6; transcript template).** Before the learner answers, write the expected answer and the named wrong models into the transcript; judge the reply against them as took / did not take before composing any response; never accept "makes sense" as a pass. Consider a separate grader context for the final unaided task. — [Arvin 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10297); [Kasneci & Kasneci 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14604); [Daheim et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09136); [arXiv:2601.08843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08843)
7. **Re-aim plan approval (`learning-plan` §4).** Ask the learner to confirm (a) the goal, stated as something they will do unaided, and (b) which nodes they really hold, instead of asking them to validate a dependency structure they cannot yet evaluate. — [Karich et al. 2014](https://edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05); [Sitzmann & Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/); [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)

**Add**

1. **A practice/review skill with delayed, varied retrieval**, as LearnHarness has. When a later session pre-reads the graph, probe prior nodes with a retrieval question before treating them as held. — [Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); [LearnHarness README](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness)
2. **A self-monitoring prompt before feedback.** Have the learner state confidence or predict whether they are right before the tutor judges, so monitoring is not fully offloaded. (`learning-teach` already allows "optional thinking/confidence"; make it routine on checks.) — [Fan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544); [Darvishi et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/99e9a0ee-4feb-405c-8e66-062c6b67aa11)
3. **A guard on step size.** Add a note that steps finer than one meaningful inference bring no further gain; fade check frequency for material the learner is clearly ahead on. — [VanLehn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369); [Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136)
4. **Honest framing in README/theory.** State that the method is distilled from an uncited personal theory with no outcome data, and that expected benefit relative to careful self-study is unproven. — [LearnHarness docs index](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learnharness/blob/main/docs/README.md); [Weidlich et al. 2025](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/chatgpt-in-education-an-effect-in-search-of-a-cause/)

### Inferences
- Priority order by expected harm avoided: change 3 and 4 (persistent unverified claims) first, because they are the only items where the system can make the learner durably worse off and where every successful trial did something the repo does not; then change 2 and 6 (false completion); then change 1 and add 1 (larger learning gain).
- None of these changes conflicts with the repo's "agent-agnostic, no app" constraint. All are text edits to SKILL.md files and one or two schema fields; only source grounding needs a tool (web or user-supplied files), and it can degrade gracefully to "marked unverified".
- For the Heidegger-type use case specifically, the claim-type distinction (what the text says / what scholars say / what is disputed) is likely worth more than any generic hallucination mitigation, because the main risk is a confident, fluent, single reading being stored as fact.

### Gaps
- None of these recommendations has been tested on this system; they transfer findings from school and university STEM settings to an adult, self-directed, chat-based, partly humanities use case.
- The size of benefit from each change is unknown. The only quantified analogue is Bastani's contrast between guarded and unguarded tutors (17% worse vs roughly level on an unaided exam).
- Sources flagged above as "secondary only" or "not re-fetched" should be opened before their numbers are quoted in a final report: VanLehn's per-category effects and "interaction plateau" wording, Ma et al.'s g for large-group instruction, Karich et al.'s g = 0.05, Pardos & Bhandari's self-consistency figures, the misconception-congruency study's authorship, the Bartoš et al. preprint, and the Wang & Fan retraction notice itself.
